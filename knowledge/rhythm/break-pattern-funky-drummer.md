# Break pattern: Funky Drummer

**Sourcing note (this session):** full-page fetch was unavailable for every
host tried (en.wikipedia.org, articles.roland.com, flypaper.soundfly.com,
drumstheword.com, drumforum.org) — research went through WebSearch
excerpts, same constraint as this topic's other break entries. The
two-bar structure and backbeat displacement are corroborated across two
independent pages (Roland's tutorial breakdown and Soundfly's Flypaper
history piece) using different wording for the same claim, which is
treated as solid corroboration rather than one source repeated. The exact
kick-drum lattice is where sources genuinely conflict — see "A named
disagreement" below — and this entry does not paper over that.

## The rule

### The two-bar shape

"The main drum part is comprised of a two-bar pattern, with a snare hit on
the two and four, a standard sort of 4/4 rock pattern, but with a simple
variation — the four beat hit in the first measure is delayed by one
eighth note" (Soundfly/Flypaper, "A Compendium of History's Most Famous
Hip-Hop Rhythms"). Read literally, this means bar 1's beat-4 snare is
pushed to the "+" of 4 while bar 2's beat-4 snare stays put — the loop
resolves rather than repeating the displacement forever, which is the
detail this entry's two-bar block is built to preserve (a 1-bar loop of
just the displaced bar would misrepresent the source).

### Hi-hat: one continuous line, two documented opens

The groove is "built upon a challenging single handed 16th note hi-hat
pattern," with "subtle single sixteenth note openings on the 'e' of beats
2 and 4, with the first hi-hat opening played on its own whilst the
second opening is played at the same time as the bass drum on the 'e' of
beat 4" (drumstheword.com-style synthesis). That coincidence — open hat
landing WITH a kick specifically on the "e" of beat 4 — is one of the few
kick positions this entry can cite directly, which is why it appears
marked sourced rather than constructed above.

### Ghost notes: present, and the hardest one is named

Stubblefield "played a large role in shaping funk drumming... with great
use of syncopation and ghost notes," and "the hardest ghost note to play
[is] the one on the 'a' of beat 3 because it is immediately followed by
the accented note on beat 4" (drumforum.org-aggregated synthesis). This
entry places that specific ghost at 3.75 in both bars; the rest of the
ghost-note inventory (there are more ghosts in the real recording than any
excerpt this session named individually) is filled in by construction, not
citation.

### A named disagreement: does the kick land on beat 1?

This is the real conflict in this session's sourcing, not a smoothed-over
detail. One search result (Roland's tutorial breakdown) describes the
groove as bringing "the bass drum on the 1, and '&' then '&' and 'e'" —
i.e., a kick ON beat 1. A separately-aggregated characterization says
flatly "Clyde doesn't play bass drums on beats 1 and 3, creating a funky
groove with no let up" — i.e., NO kick on beat 1. These cannot both be
literally true of the same performance. This entry follows the
"no kick on 1 or 3" reading as primary (it's the more specific, rule-shaped
claim, and matches the way funk pedagogy generally credits this groove
for displacing the kick off the obvious downbeats) and marks the beat-1
and beat-2.5 kicks in the notation above as `CONSTRUCTED` rather than
sourced BECAUSE of this conflict — an honest reader should feel free to
remove them and still be consistent with half the sourcing on this point.
The one kick position free of this argument is the "e" of beat 4 (4.25),
sourced separately via its coincidence with the open hi-hat.
