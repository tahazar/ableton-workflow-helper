# Rest placement: the silence that makes a drop conversational

A call-and-response pair reads as chantable and conversational because of
the rests around each phrase. This entry isolates the rest rule from the
general pairing grammar in `arrangement/call-response-drop-grammar`.

## Executable

```awh-notation
# Illustrative: deliberately sparse call/response over 2 bars, 4/4.
sig 4/4
1|1    G4   1/4   v112   # call: one short bright stab, not a full phrase
# beats 1.25-4 of bar 1 left empty: the rest is the question mark
2|1    C1   1/2   v127   # response: low stab lands squarely on beat 1
# beats 1.5-4 of bar 2 left empty: answer given, then silence before repeat
```

## The rule

- Silence is a compositional element in dubstep, not dead space to fill.
  Gaps between bass hits make each hit read heavier and give the groove its
  punch (unison.audio, "How to Make Dubstep"; cymatics.fm, "How To Make
  Dubstep: 15 Essential Tips").
- In a call/response pair, put the response's entrance a bar (or half-bar)
  after the call finishes, not right on its heels. The gap lets the ear hear
  "call ... [pause] ... response" as two statements instead of one
  continuous line (dubstepforum.com thread; pointblankmusicschool.com, "How
  to Create a Dubstep Drop from Scratch").
- Don't fill every gap with percussion or fills. The low sound's natural
  decay and the surrounding silence do rhythmic work a busier arrangement
  would need drum hits for (unison.audio).
- Test for call-and-response versus doubling: solo the two sounds and listen
  for a rest between them. No rest, no conversation, only two parts talking
  over each other (YouTube, "Why Your Drops SUCK Pt. 1: Call and Response").
