# Rest placement: the silence that makes a drop conversational

A call-and-response pair reads as "chantable"/conversational because of
the rests around each phrase, not despite them. This entry isolates the
rest/silence rule from the general pairing grammar in
`arrangement/call-response-drop-grammar`.

## Executable

```awh-notation
# ILLUSTRATIVE — deliberately sparse call/response over 2 bars, 4/4.
sig 4/4
1|1    G4   1/4   v112   # call: one short bright stab, not a full phrase
# beats 1.25-4 of bar 1 left empty — the rest IS the question mark
2|1    C1   1/2   v127   # response: low stab lands squarely on beat 1
# beats 1.5-4 of bar 2 left empty — answer given, then silence before repeat
```

## The rule

- Silence is a compositional element in dubstep, not dead space to be
  filled: gaps between bass hits are what make each hit read as heavier
  and give the groove its punch (unison.audio, "How to Make Dubstep";
  cymatics.fm, "How To Make Dubstep: 15 Essential Tips").
- For a call/response pair specifically, put the response's entrance a
  bar (or half-bar) after the call finishes rather than immediately on
  its heels — the gap is what lets the ear register "call ... [pause] ...
  response" as two separate statements instead of one continuous line
  (dubstepforum.com thread; pointblankmusicschool.com, "How to Create a
  Dubstep Drop from Scratch").
- Don't rush to fill every gap with percussion or fills — let the low
  sound's natural decay and the surrounding silence do rhythmic work that
  a busier arrangement would otherwise need drum hits for (unison.audio).
- Practical test for whether a pair reads as call-and-response rather than
  doubling: solo the two candidate sounds and listen for an actual rest
  between them. No rest, no "conversation" — just two parts talking over
  each other (YouTube, "Why Your Drops SUCK Pt. 1: Call and Response").
