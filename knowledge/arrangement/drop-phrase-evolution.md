# Drop phrase evolution across 16-32 bars

How a call/response pair (see `arrangement/call-response-drop-grammar`)
should change across a full drop instead of looping unchanged for 16-32
bars.

## Executable

```awh-notation
# ILLUSTRATIVE 4-bar excerpt of a 16-bar bar-map, not a transcription.
sig 4/4
# Bars 1-2: state the pair plainly, no variation — let it land as a hook
1|1   G4   1/2  v110
1|3   G4   1/2  v105
2|1   C1   1/2  v127
2|3   C1   1/4  v118

# Bars 3-4: repeat verbatim once more before touching anything
3|1   G4   1/2  v110
3|3   G4   1/2  v105
4|1   C1   1/2  v127
4|3   C1   1/4  v118
# (bars 5-8 in the full map: vary the CALL only, response unchanged —
#  see "The rule" below; bars 9-12: add a counter-melody under the
#  response; bars 13-16: full drop-variation pass or handoff to the next
#  section)
```

## The rule

- Trap arrangement convention treats the drop itself as 16-32 bars, and
  commonly writes that span as two 16-bar halves, or as a base "drop" plus
  an optional "drop variation" section later, rather than one static loop
  (edmprod.com, "How To Make Trap Music: 7 Steps To Hard-Hitting Beats";
  ourmusicworld.com, "How to Structure a Trap Beat").
- To keep a repeating call/response loop from going stale across that
  span, change ONE side of the pair at a time rather than rewriting both
  together: e.g. vary the call's rhythm or add a counter-melody in the
  second half while leaving the response's low sound as a fixed anchor —
  the fixed side is what keeps the loop recognizable as the same drop
  (elizabethrecords.net, "The Ultimate Guide to Making Trap Beats";
  cymatics.fm, "EDM Song Structure: Turn Your Loop Into A Song!").
- A full second drop-variation — a new arrangement pass over the same
  call/response sound palette — is the standard device for a later
  chorus-equivalent, so the hook returns at least twice without being
  looped unchanged for the whole track (ourmusicworld.com; cymatics.fm).
- Micro-tension devices for keeping a repeating phrase alive bar-to-bar:
  a one-bar break, or dropping an element (e.g. the kick) at the end of
  an 8-bar phrase and reintroducing it right as the ear would start
  tuning the loop out — a reset cue timed to phrase boundaries, not
  random (edmprod.com, "The Advanced Guide to Tension and Energy in
  Electronic Music").
